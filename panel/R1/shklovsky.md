Verdict: OBJECT
Observed: The sleeve is made strange (index.png): "Groovy" in stacked-stripe type, a vinyl whose groove is real code. But the strangeness stops at the home page. On a3-collections.png and b6-power-assert-m.png the track pages are calm, ordinary highlighted code beside a dark output box. The one thing that is actually unfamiliar to a learner, Groovy's own idiom (the spread *., closures with implicit it, GString, power assert's pipe diagram), is rendered with the same weight as println. The spiral code is also unreadable at record size, so it is texture, not perception. A literal LP skin is itself a stock 70s device: automatized nostalgia.
Anchor: Art as Technique: the device of defamiliarization exists to impede perception and prolong the act of seeing; habitual perception "devours" the object.
Meaning: I see a warm record-shop world that introduces Groovy as a pleasant album, with the strangeness spent on the cover.
Artist question: Where on the track page does the reader's eye have to slow down?
Neutral question: What does a learner do differently on A3 than on any other docs page?
Opinion: The decoration is nostalgic and fast to read; the difficult idiom is not roughened. The code is the stone, and it is not made stony.
Requested change: On each track page, add a "needle drop" interaction on the code block: clicking a line (or the 1-18 gutter number) un-fuses it into its Java-equivalent form beside it (e.g. line 8 `albums*.title` expands to `albums.collect { it.title }` then to a Java for-loop), shown in slowed, staged steps. Roughen the idiom by making the learner step through the compression, rather than reading it at a glance.
